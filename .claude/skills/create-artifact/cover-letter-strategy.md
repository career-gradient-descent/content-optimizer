# Cover Letter Strategy

## The governing rule

The résumé is evidence; the cover letter is argument. It does the one job the résumé cannot: it says, in your own voice, why *this* role and *this* person meet, through a concrete story and a connecting thesis, not a re-narration of the résumé. A reviewer who finishes it understands something the bullets could only imply.

Same split as the résumé:

- **Voice is anchored** to career.md's About and the writing-style rule, warmed half a degree for a letter: contractions are fine, the register is a notch warmer, substance over packaging holds. The anti-patterns rule applies unchanged.
- **The argument is the opportunity's.** Which story opens it, which facets it connects, which of the company's real situation it names: driven by the JD and `research.md`.

## The shape

One page. Three short paragraphs.

- **Open on a true, concrete thing you did that this role evokes** — not "I am writing to apply." A specific experience, named plainly, that makes the fit read as discovered: *"Early in my career I spent a year inside a wealth-management fintech..."* The opening earns the read or loses it.
- **Connect two or three facets to the role's core.** Name the specific work — a project, a system, a decision — that maps to what the role actually needs, in your register.
- **Close low-key:** availability and an open door. *"I'm Melbourne-based with full Australian working rights. I'd love to talk."* No flourish.

## What lives here, not on the résumé

The letter is the controlled channel for what is true and compelling but tonally wrong for a cold résumé scan:

- The *why* behind the work — a design philosophy, the through-line across projects, the reason a role's framing resonates.
- Connective narrative the résumé can't hold — how one role led to the next, what a run of side projects is really about.
- A project named to carry the argument, even one kept off the résumé. Name it for what it demonstrates.

## Grounding — every claim, prose included

The letter narrates your history exactly as career.md records it. This binds narrative, not only numbers: which role came first, how long you were somewhere, what you *owned* versus *contributed to*, what you are *drawn to*. The persuasion is in the selection and framing of real things, never in nudging a fact to fit.

## Tailoring is the point; reverse-engineering is not

A cover letter is inherently tailored — that is its purpose, so naming the company's real situation and the role's genuine language is right and good. The anti-patterns rule still holds: reference what you genuinely know (from research) and what you genuinely are (from career.md); never echo JD phrasing you can't stand behind.

## Mechanics

- One page, via the cover-letter template.
- Date present: the date of writing.
- Subject is the role title.
- Addressee: the company or its hiring team. For an agency-mediated role (per `research.md`'s channel finding), the consultant reads it first — keep it fit-forward and readable, and do not address a client you cannot confirm.
- Letterhead and closing carry the résumé's contact set and voice.
